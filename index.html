Voting Reform in Canada

How much does First Past the Post distort the vote?

Under First Past the Post, a party's share of the seats in the House of Commons can look very different from its share of the vote. In each of the last four federal elections, the party that formed government won a much larger share of seats than of votes.

Election Most seats Its share of the vote Its share of seats Most votes

During the 2015 campaign, the Liberal Party promised that it would be the last federal election held under First Past the Post. A special committee of the House of Commons studied the alternatives in 2016, and the government dropped the commitment in February 2017. Every federal election since has used First Past the Post.

How does First Past the Post work?

First Past the Post (FPTP) is Canada’s current electoral system. Voters in each riding vote for one candidate in that riding. Whoever has the most votes is elected as the MP for that riding. — Samara
Under alternative vote (AV), each riding elects one Member of Parliament (MP). Voters rank candidates in order of preference. The winning candidate must have the support of a majority of voters in the riding, if necessary via a transfer of votes from eliminated candidates. — Samara

How the Alternative Vote simulation works →

Dual-member proportional representation (DMP) is a voting system designed to produce proportional election results across a region by electing two representatives in each of the region’s districts. The 1st seat in every district is awarded to the candidate who receives the most votes, similar to first-past-the-post voting (FPTP). The 2nd seat is awarded to one of the remaining district candidates so that proportionality is achieved across the region, using a calculation that aims to award parties their seats in the districts where they had their strongest performances. — Wikipedia

How the Dual Member Proportional simulation works →

Mixed-member proportional representation (MMP) is mixed or “hybrid” because it combines elements of proportional and single-member plurality systems. With MMP, voters have a single MP who represents their riding, while other seats are distributed proportionately to total votes cast in the election. Voters have two votes: one for a candidate running in their riding, and a second for a party or a candidate on a party list. — Samara

How the Mixed Member Proportional simulation works →

The single transferable vote (STV) combines elements of different systems to both achieve a relatively proportional result and to elect MPs from specific constituencies. It has ranked ballots and large ridings, each with multiple elected MPs. — Samara

Under FPTP, each riding elects one MP.

Under DMP, each riding would have been twice as big, and would have contained two local MPs.

Under MMP, provinces are split up into regions. Regions have local MPs who each represent a single riding, as well as regional MPs who represent the entire region. In the chart below, each grouping of seats within a province represents a region (R-1 through R-35). Seats with a border are local MPs; seats without one are regional MPs.

Under AV, ridings boundaries would have remained the same, with one MP per riding.

Under STV, ridings would have been merged into larger districts, each electing several MPs.

Sources

Actual election results, including each party's share of the vote, are from the official voting results published by Elections Canada for the 2015, 2019, 2021 and 2025 general elections. Parties other than the five listed, and independent candidates, are grouped as “Other”.

Results under the other voting systems for 2015 are simulations supplied by contributors. Mixed Member Proportional results were provided by Wilf Day. Alternative Vote results were provided by Éric Grenier. Dual Member Proportional results were provided by Rhys Goldstein. Single Transferable Vote results were provided by Ryan Campbell.

Results under the other voting systems for 2019, 2021 and 2025 are simulations run for this site, following the 2015 methods as closely as possible. They use second-choice polling published by Innovative Research Group during each campaign, and riding boundary files from Elections Canada. The assumptions are set out in How the simulations work, and the Python code and data are on GitHub.
